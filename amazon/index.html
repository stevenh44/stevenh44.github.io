One worldwide labor planner for grocery delivery

Replaced hand-run, region-specific Excel labor planning with one platform across NA and EU: $0.41 off cost per delivery, ops time per run down 83%, and a roadmap sized at $4M+ a year.

The problem

Amazon grocery delivery in North America and Europe partly runs on Delivery Service Partners: contracted companies supplying drivers in scheduled shifts. Their labor was locked in a week ahead at 100% of forecast, but grocery demand doesn’t hold still for a week. A high forecast meant idle drivers and higher cost per delivery. A low one cost customers same-day windows.

The stopgap was region-specific Excel tools for adding labor intraday. They proved the concept but were unsupported, outside the core tech org, and manual: about 30 minutes of central operations time per run, many times a day, on two continents.

The decision

The obvious move was to extend Amazon’s gig-driver labor planners. We chose a standalone platform instead. Partners plan labor differently: legal shift minimums, changes sent to a company rather than a driver’s app, and capacity set by another system. The owning team also couldn’t meet our timeline. Building separately was faster and kept integration open for later.

I owned the business requirements, rollout, and roadmap, working with engineering, science, central operations, and program managers in both regions.

What shipped

  • MVP in October 2021 at parity with the spreadsheets, then a shadow-mode rollout: 16 runs a day alongside the old process, checked against actual outcomes before any site cut over.
  • 15 sites by December, 18 by mid-February 2022, 24 by end of March (10 NA, 14 EU, including the US, Italy, and France). The first single worldwide tool for partner labor planning. Intraday came first, with week-ahead tools as the next roadmap phase.
  • Direct integrations with capacity and scheduling systems, replacing a manual CSV upload, the most error-prone step.
  • Full logging of inputs, calculations, and outputs, with dashboards explaining each recommendation to program managers.
  • Exception handling and first-version dynamic productivity rates, added after watching operators use the tool.

Results

  • $0.41 lower cost per delivery (250 bps) from the integrations alone.
  • 83% less operations time per run, from 30 minutes to under 5: roughly 15 hours of manual work saved a day across both regions.
  • Requested-vs-provided labor mismatches, previously routine, nearly eliminated.
  • A 2022 roadmap I built and sequenced, sized at $4M+ in annual savings, mainly from better driver utilization.

What I’d do differently

Re-cut the roadmap when scope moves, not at year end. We planned to ship week-ahead scheduling the same year and didn’t. I underestimated the external integration effort, and exception handling outgrew its scope. That work paid off as a core input at every planning horizon, but the roadmap should have changed the moment it started growing.
