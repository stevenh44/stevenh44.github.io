Revel

Scaled an all-electric rideshare fleet from 50 to 500 cars and 200 to 1,200+ drivers, founded a $1.5M/month leasing line, and ran product and design for the company.

The business

Rideshare was Revel’s core business: an all-electric fleet in New York City, with company-owned Teslas and drivers on payroll. Alongside it, Revel ran shared mopeds and public EV charging hubs. I joined in 2022 at about 50 cars and 200 drivers. By the end it had 500 cars, 1,200+ drivers (1,400 at peak), a leasing line doing $1.5M a month, and an operating platform we built ourselves. Revel shut down rideshare in August 2025. I was there through the last day.

Owning the fleet and employing drivers put every cost a marketplace pushes onto contractors on Revel’s books: idle time, keys, insurance, depots, payroll. Most of my work turned one of those costs into a product decision.

How the org worked

I joined as the rideshare PM and left running product and design for the company.

Written specs as the operating system. Every project started as a PRD in a shared database: context, success criteria, stakeholders, scope, risks, launch plan. I wrote over a hundred. The team wrote hundreds more. The template stayed light, because a PRD that takes a quarter to write doesn’t get read. Each PRD linked to its engineering RFCs and its Figma files, so spec, technical plan and design were three linked documents, not three meetings.

Pods as the unit of capacity. Engineering ran as named pods: PitCrew for the driver app and ops console, Commodore for dispatch, IRS (internal rideshare systems) for onboarding, eligibility and pay plumbing, the Rental Team for leasing, and Algos for matching. Every PRD named the pods it needed, so a roadmap was a list of pods and quarters, not a list of wishes.

A business line, founded and handed off. Revel Leasing came out of the move to 1099: contractors lease the car they drive. I built the case, wrote the foundations (lease payment service, Stripe billing, DocuSign agreements) and launched it, billing the first customers by hand until engineering took over. Leasing also cut the core business’s cost by $0.92 per driver hour. I then handed the product to the PM on my team, who owned it through the rental app, invoicing, security deposits, weekly rentals, and the growth to $1.5M a month.

Product and design, company-wide. In my last stretch, every PM and designer at the company reported to me.

Also shipped

Build vs. buy. Over three years we replaced third-party tools for shift scheduling, the fleet map, vehicle controls, driver communications and insurance workflows with our own. That saved about $2.5M a year and let ops workflows change as fast as the business. Keyless entry is one chapter. The ops console is most of the rest.

A clickable reconstruction

The platform no longer exists. I rebuilt the ops console and driver app as clickable prototypes, with every driver, vehicle, license number and dollar amount fabricated. The case studies link into specific screens.
