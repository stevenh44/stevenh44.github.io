The move to 1099: from hourly wage to per-ride fare

Moving ~1,000 NYC drivers from W2 employment to per-ride 1099 contracting. Modeled at +$4.50 per driver hour, landed at about +$4. Take-home pay held, riders never noticed, and a $1.5M-a-month leasing business came out of it.

The short version

Performance pay took W2 as far as it could go, and Revel still carried all the utilization risk (the W2 story). In 2024 we moved ~1,000 NYC drivers to 1099 contracting, paid per ride. The payroll-tax burden shifts to the contractor, so if take-home pay holds, margin improves, incentives align, and riders never notice. We modeled +$4.50 revenue per driver hour. It landed at +$3.50 at launch and about +$4.00 after tuning.

Why now

  • The business: nearly every driver hour lost money, and the levers we were pulling weren’t closing the gap.
  • The drivers: for many, Revel was their first W2 job, and withholding left no margin. A cohort was explicitly asking to be 1099.

Why it was hard

On W2, one HR and payroll platform handled payroll and tax filing, identity, classification, background checks, payouts and benefits. On 1099, each of those jobs needed a replacement, and half of what we needed had no W2 equivalent.

How we piloted

We launched the pilot within a month, with 15 drivers and minimal engineering: hourly pay off, ride rejection on, key metrics measured. The first cohort ran on Google Sheets I built and maintained with the ops team. W2 and 1099 then ran side by side for six months while we validated the economics and driver experience and built the platform. By month seven, every driver had converted.

I ran the program as a single pod across HR, legal, marketing, PR, ops, engineering and data. My job was sequencing: what had to happen, in what order, owned by whom. Engineering reorganized around it. I kept a small full-stack task force on the migration, and the senior PM on my team ran the larger team building the 1099 product.

What we built

Restrictions, the eligibility primitive. One open restriction keeps a driver offline. A configurable set attaches at conversion or onboarding, and each clears itself when its check passes. Ops gets visibility, not an override, and Revel never took ownership of drivers’ SSNs. Built for conversion, it became the whole onboarding funnel. The onboarding case study →

A fare instead of a wage. With the data team, who carried much of the modeling, we set per-ride pay with a linear regression on trip time and distance, balancing acceptance against earnings per hour. The metric we watched hardest was pay per minute, estimated versus actual.

A payments source of truth, and payouts within the hour. Every trip and tip writes a row with amount, type, timestamps, and a plain-language description that doubles as the per-ride earnings disclosure regulators require. Each row becomes a Branch disbursement with a unique transaction ID, so nothing pays twice.

What surprised us

  • A silent identity gap. Our tax-identity vendor verified drivers but never wrote a unique ID back to our system. Nothing failed loudly. It surfaced months later as 1099 tax-document failures for the first ~50 drivers, and cleanup took weeks across engineering, product and HR.
  • Some drivers declined or switched back. W2 remained an option until the legal notice period closed it. About 150 drivers left over the transition, with no impact on service.
  • The expected backlash never came. PR prepared for a driver and press reaction that never materialized, largely because take-home pay held.

Results

  • +$4 revenue per driver hour, against a model of +$4.50, realized within about eight months
  • Driver take-home pay stable throughout, the constraint that made the bet defensible
  • No impact on passenger satisfaction or demand coverage
  • $1.5M/month in Revel Leasing revenue by month seven: a new business line, not just a cost shift
  • Pay cycle: weekly → within an hour of each ride or tip

What I’d do differently

Trace every integration end to end before scaling. The pilot ran without end-to-end integrations, so nothing flagged the missing identity write-back until tax season. I’d verify what each vendor writes back before the first driver runs through it.

Instrument the pilot’s seams. We judged the driver transition on ops anecdotes and take-home pay. Nothing major slipped through, but I’d want instrumented signals, not stories.
