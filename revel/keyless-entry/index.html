Keyless entry for a 500-car fleet

The product and workflow that made a driver's phone the key: remote assignment, console controls, and an in-app key scoped to the shift. Retired a 24/7 role at three depots, cut four minutes of dead time per shift, and put the car's controls in the ops console, next to the driver's record, instead of in a separate Tesla app.

The short version

Revel drivers started every shift by collecting a physical key from a staffed desk. With 500 Teslas and more depots opening, that cost grew with every location. I led the product and workflow that made the driver’s phone the key: remote vehicle assignment, vehicle controls in the ops console, and an in-app key (unlock, start, lock, find) valid only for the assigned car during a live shift. Tesla’s API handled the car. We built everything else: assignment, permissions, the driver and ops flows, and rollout. Vehicle handoff went from a staffed counter to a text message, and ops no longer needed a separate Tesla app to help a driver.

The problem

Each depot was staffed around the clock, largely to hand out keys: about two people per depot per day across the ground and driver operations teams. That headcount scaled linearly with depots, and the roles were hard to hire and retain.

The keys themselves were a recurring problem: lost, taken home at end of shift, a security exposure in a drawer. Remote lock and unlock ran through Standard Fleet, a paid third-party tool, though Tesla’s API could already do everything a key does.

Three phases, each useful on its own

Phase 1: the app is the key. Ops got vehicle controls in the console: unlock, lock, remote start, trunk, horn, and lights. Drivers got the same controls in their app, scoped to the assigned car while the shift was in progress. Horn and lights solve a practical problem: finding one white Tesla in a lot full of them. Ground ops still assigned cars in person but had nothing to hand over. Ops also stopped toggling between our console and a separate Tesla app, because the car’s controls now sat next to the driver’s record.

Phase 2: ops assigns remotely. With the key gone, the depot staffer’s only job was naming the car, so we moved that to the console. Drivers check in with a QR code and wait in “standby.” A driver-ops agent, increasingly remote, assigns a vehicle, with ineligible cars grayed out and the reason shown. The driver gets a text with the plate and a link straight to the car’s controls. On its own this phase changed little, but paired with the app it removed the last reason to staff the counter.

Phase 3: assignment automated. A scheduled job assigned a car to each driver in the standby queue. Agents handled only exceptions, such as a car that failed its checks or a driver who needed a specific model. Assignment went from someone’s all-day task to a queue that emptied itself.

Target throughout: 95% of drivers on depot-based Teslas never touch a physical key to start, work, or finish a shift.

Testing before rollout

Before any driver used it, we sent a harmless command, flash the lights, to the fleet on a schedule. Not every command arrived, and the failure pattern led us to test sleeping vehicles before the pilot rather than during it.

We then piloted with a few trusted drivers on beta builds, then about 25 drivers at one depot. Two failures surfaced that no API documentation covered:

  • Sleeping cars. A Tesla parked for a while went into deep sleep, and the unlock command hung. Drivers found that jiggling the door handle woke the car. We redesigned the flow so the app explained what was happening and what to do, instead of spinning.
  • Garage dead zones. Cars on lower parking levels had no cellular signal. The fix was Wi-Fi coverage on every level before rollout there.

Found with 25 drivers instead of 1,200, both became a UI change and a facilities ticket rather than a failed launch.

The pilot also exposed flow problems. Drivers had to open a car-details screen to unlock and start, back out, then begin the shift-start flow, and about half hesitated. A teammate sized it: ten seconds across 1,200 drivers, at our revenue per driver hour, was meaningful annual revenue for a cheap fix. Drivers also weren’t told when their car was unassigned or swapped. The fixes: a linear flow, a text on every assignment change, and car swaps without ending the driver’s shift.

Lockout risk

If the app is the key, a bug is a lockout. I flagged that risk up front, and the fallback was deliberate: ops could always unlock remotely, first through that tool and then through our own console controls.

Results

  • Retired the 24/7 key-distribution role at three depots. One around-the-clock position is roughly four full-time hires.
  • Four minutes less non-revenue time per driver shift on average, from check-in to ride availability. With about 1,200 drivers averaging 22 hours a week, that moved well over a hundred driver-hours a week from paid waiting to ride-eligible time.
  • Depots could open without a key-desk staffing plan, and driver support could be remote.
  • Most drivers could walk in, find their car, and drive off without speaking to anyone.

Why it mattered

Once a car no longer needed a person beside it, renters could pick up without help from the ground operations team, and that team could focus on cleaning and charging the fleet. Revel Leasing, the rental product that grew to $1.5M a month, depended on exactly that.
