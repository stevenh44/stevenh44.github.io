Dispatch: matching drivers to rides when you own the fleet

Batch matching that cut about a minute from average wait, a price on empty pickup time, and a matcher that knows what a toll costs. Dispatch looks different when every idle hour is on your P&L.

The short version

On Uber, the driver bears the cost of waiting between rides. Revel owned the cars and, for most of this period, employed the drivers, so every empty minute was Revel’s cost. Over about a year I ran the cross-functional pod that moved matching from first-come-first-served to batched assignment. Underneath it sat a synthetic ETA that let the matcher weigh costs like non-revenue pickup time and tolls, not just time. Average passenger wait fell by about a minute, and the fleet spent less time driving to the wrong place.

Batch matching

The original matcher gave each accepted ride to the nearest free driver, instantly. That failed predictably: the first request took a car that the next one, thirty seconds later, needed more.

We made matching asynchronous. Accepted rides wait a few seconds in a “matching” state, then every ride in the window is assigned at once with the best overall pairing. The passenger sees a brief “finding your driver” screen instead of an instant match.

Two decisions shaped the launch:

  • Start the window at 1–10 seconds, though efficiency gains only appear at 20–30. The first release proved a visible pause didn’t raise cancellations before we asked for gains.
  • Measure deadheading, not speed. The metric was recall deadheading, cars driving empty to a pickup. A window that makes matching slower and deadheading shorter is a win.

Batching also let the matcher weigh more than distance: a driver due for a break or heading back to the depot, a first-time rider who deserved the most reliable match, a higher-revenue ride. We projected a 10–20% cut in total wait time at peak from batching alone.

My team built nearly all of it: the batching engine, the assignment logic, and the synthetic ETA. The consumer team built the waiting screen.

Synthetic ETA

A matcher optimizes one number, usually ETA. But much of what made a pairing good or bad for Revel wasn’t time: a toll on the route, a car pulled out of the busy core, dead miles nobody paid for.

Synthetic ETA was the real ETA plus time-equivalent penalties for those costs. If a toll counted as +2 minutes, a toll-free car a minute further away would win. Passengers were still quoted the real ETA. Every cost we cared about became a knob the matcher already understood.

Dead-time pricing

Pickup time was consistently about 20% of available driver time, none of it revenue. The maximum ETA shown to a passenger was also the maximum dead time we accepted, so a driver could spend twenty minutes reaching a pickup mid-surge. A bad ride could also pull a car out of the core, where zone pricing then discounted the ride that brought demand back.

I wrote the spec that defined non-revenue pickup time as distinct from wait time. A 20-minute pickup that includes 15 minutes dropping off a previous passenger is only 5 minutes of dead time. The spec weighed three options: cap dead time and show “no drivers” past a demand-based threshold, charge a premium past a threshold, or penalize dead time in the batch matcher. The matcher fix had a cost: matching time could rise from 7 seconds to 45 for the worst 20% of rides.

We shipped the last two: a price premium once dead time passed a threshold, and dead time weighted into the synthetic ETA so the matcher avoided long empty pickups when a better pairing existed.

Toll-aware matching

About 9% of Revel’s toll spend, $3,500–4,500 a week, came from drivers crossing a toll to reach a pickup, because the matcher didn’t know tolls existed. A Brooklyn driver sent through the Hugh L. Carey Tunnel to a Manhattan pickup costs $7.46 with E-ZPass (2026 rates) before the ride starts, which can be most of the margin on a short fare.

The fix was narrow: move routing to an API version that returns toll data, and add the toll to the synthetic ETA for any pairing that crosses one. It was deliberately a tiebreak, not a rule. If the only free car is across the bridge, the passenger still gets it. Launch plan: watch cancellation rate, service levels, and wait times.

Supply positioning

With employee drivers, a rebalance sent a driver to a high-demand area. Contractors couldn’t be directed that way, and they drifted toward Manhattan and the airports. We replaced instruction with information: a demand heat map in the driver app, built on a weighted rolling average so it showed where rides would be, not where the last spike had been. Success meant utilization held once we stopped directing drivers.

Results

  • ~60 seconds off average passenger wait from batch matching
  • One mechanism, synthetic ETA, carrying tolls, dead time, and business constraints into every match, instead of a rule for each
  • Fewer tolls on pickups and shorter empty pickups (directional)
