StoryCorps — 75,000 interviews, one public archive

Migrated a national oral-history archive from Drupal to WordPress, joining facilitated and app-recorded interviews in one public archive, and wrote the verification script that took field-level accuracy from ~80% to 98%. Then extended the CRM to individual donors, participants, and interviews.

The short version

StoryCorps records conversations between ordinary people and preserves them at the Library of Congress. I ran product for its digital side: the public archive, the staff recording and cataloguing systems, and the CRM underneath the organization.

The headline project was the archive migration: 75,000 professionally recorded interviews moved from an aging Drupal site to a new WordPress archive. I managed the engineers building it and wrote the script that verified their work field by field. Measured accuracy rose from about 80% to 98%, and the archive team went from overlooked to co-owner.

The move also ended a split collection. Interviews recorded with the StoryCorps App already fed the WordPress archive, while facilitated interviews lived in Drupal. Migrating the facilitated collection put both in one public archive.

Verifying the migration

The old and new systems disagreed, and nothing measured by how much. Each interview-participant pair carried about 110 fields, from release restrictions and keyword taxonomies to birthplace and self-described identity. “Correct” was defined by the archive team that had curated those fields by hand for years.

The script pulled a year of records from each side, normalized differences that weren’t errors (an email versus a name, keyword order, encoding), and keyed every row on interview and participant ID. It wrote a workbook showing each field three times: old value, new value, and a 1 or 0. Every mismatch was countable and traceable to a cause.

# for each comparison column (every third) write an if statement that
# compares the two left columns. Write 1 if they're the same, 0 otherwise.
worksheet.write_formula(row, col, "=IF(" + old_cell + "=" + new_cell + ",1,0)")

The workbook let the Recording & Archives team find systemic import errors: whenever the new archive disagreed with the old one, some part of the data hadn’t transferred completely. I worked each fix with the engineers: trace the field through the mapping, fix, test, re-run. Leadership got a number that meant something. The loop ran until the match rate reached 98% and the remaining 2% was known, explained, and accepted.

I delayed the archive launch to get there, because sync accuracy mattered more than the date. The extra time also cut how long a new interview took to reach the archive from five weeks to two. Two thousand lines of Python turned “trust us” into “let’s check together.”

Returning interviews to participants

The second half of the launch let 125,000+ participants find and claim their interviews. I wrote the publication rules as code. Sensitive-content notes, production interviews, certain program prefixes, missing general releases, and participants under thirteen kept an interview locked. Everything else went behind a walled garden. The script validated emails and built one outreach list per participant, with a claim button for each interview, sent through Mailchimp. 98% of participants were reached, a quarter claimed their interviews, and archive traffic rose 165% year over year.

Going remote in March 2020

StoryCorps’ model was two people in a booth with a facilitator. In March 2020 every booth closed, and in under three weeks we shipped two virtual products. StoryCorps Virtual, facilitated and open source, let staff facilitators keep recording remotely. StoryCorps Connect was self-serve: two people record a video conversation from their own browsers, preserved as audio plus a photo at the Library of Congress. I pushed to ship the facilitated product first, so the staff whose work was interviewing had something to do in week one.

Connect runs inside the newly migrated archive, on the structures the migration had made trustworthy. Building on that platform, rather than standing up something separate, is why it shipped in weeks. Together the two products recorded 10,000+ interviews while the booths were closed.

I didn’t build One Small Step, StoryCorps’ program that pairs strangers with different political views for a 50-minute conversation (7,000+ people across all 50 states so far). Some of the underlying technology my boss and I built for Connect was reused to run it.

Bringing individuals into the CRM

StoryCorps’ Salesforce was well configured for institutional donors: foundations, grants, and corporate partners. Individual donors had almost no setup, and participants and interviews didn’t exist in it at all. So the organization couldn’t see its likeliest individual donors: people who had recorded something that mattered to them.

I extended the instance so a person was one record, whether they gave, recorded, or both, with interviews as their own records linked to the people in them. Development got its first view of individual giving alongside the archive.

After deduplicating years of records, we synced 500,000 contacts to marketing and built automated grant-reporting dashboards for the executive team. Individual donations rose 10%.

Building the internal tooling

I built most of the internal automation myself: Zapier and Python for integrations, AWS Lambda for unattended jobs, Salesforce Process Builder for CRM logic, Mailchimp and WordPress for participants. The marketing and archive teams got hours back every week. One file-mover script organized thousands of interviews, a few weeks of manual effort otherwise.

Why it matters

At StoryCorps, a data-model decision determined whether a family in Tulsa could still find their grandfather’s voice after we moved it. I still think about that when deciding what a dispatcher’s screen should show. StoryCorps does work that matters on a nonprofit’s budget, and it was a privilege to build for them.
